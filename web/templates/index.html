Upload an object

Send a closed (watertight) .obj model. We treat it as a hollow metal cavity and compute its lowest optical modes.

One thing to know before you trust a number: the walls are modelled as a perfect conductor. That is an excellent approximation for a real metal at microwave frequencies and a rough one at optical frequencies, where the field penetrates the metal and the true resonance shifts. Treat these frequencies as the ideal-cavity answer, not a measured one.

{# The mode selector. Each `.mode-detail` fieldset names the modes it belongs to in `data-modes`; the script at the bottom of this file shows exactly those and keeps the `required` attributes in step with what is visible -- a hidden file input that stays `required` blocks submission with a browser message pointing at a control the owner cannot see, which is the could-not-ask shape in a form. Only controls marked `data-required` are ever made required: the optional ones (scale, feature size) stay optional whichever arm is showing. #}
{# The upload itself, shared by the two arms that take an object. #}

The scale factor multiplies the model before anything else looks at it, so 1000 turns a model drawn in millimetres into one 1000 times larger. Leave it blank for the model exactly as it is. If your .obj has no stated units, this is how you say how big it really is — and the size you state is the size whose modes you get.

The refractive index is the medium filling your object: 1.0 for air or vacuum, about 1.5 for glass. More modes means a finer mesh and a longer solve; the mesh grows in proportion to this number.

Leave the feature size on auto for the default resolution, which is set by the physics of the modes you asked for. If your object has fine detail the modes depend on — a thin wall, a narrow gap — enter its size and pick its unit from the menu beside it. The feature's unit is its own and is independent of the units you uploaded your object in: 0.2 mm means two hundred microns whether your .obj is in metres or in millimetres. Setting it larger than the automatic value has no effect: the mesh is never made coarser than the detail you name.

{# The photonic-crystal arm. No file: the request is the geometry of the unit cell and the two indices. Every field here is dimensionless, which is why there is no unit menu and no scale factor -- ω comes back in units of a/(2πc) and the lattice constant never needs a number. #}

A 2-D photonic crystal: a square or triangular lattice of rods in a background, solved on the high-symmetry path through the Brillouin zone. The result is a band diagram — frequency against k — with any band gaps shaded and their midgap ratios printed. Everything here is a fraction of the lattice constant a, so nothing is stated in metres.

TM is the field out of the plane (Ez) and TE is its magnetic counterpart (Hz) — they are two different problems and are solved separately. More divisions means a finer cell and a longer solve; more bands means a larger eigenvalue problem. The app refuses a request that would exceed its node or k-point ceiling, and says which limit was hit.

{# The time-domain arm. The object comes from the shared upload fieldset above; this is the source, the run and the probes. Every field name here is read by web/td_form.py, which refuses by name -- including the ones the chosen source does not use, which are hidden by `data-when` and ignored by the form. #}

A time-domain run: we put your object on a grid, switch on a light source, and step Maxwell's equations forward in time. The result is the field recorded at each probe, its spectrum, and a short movie of the field strength. Positions are measured from the lowest corner of your object's bounding box, after the units and scale above, in the length unit you pick here.

{# One material per part when the object has several, and an optional coating for every part (filled from /parts by the script below; the pickers post part_index., part_coating. and part_coating_thickness.). #}

The movie's frames are spread evenly over this window, and the last one is at its end. A shorter window spends the same frames on less time, so it shows faster detail; the job page states the spacing in fs and in cycles of the source frequency, and says when the frames are too far apart to show the oscillation itself. At most 120 frames are kept, about a megabyte each at the largest field the grid records, and the page loads them all before playing.

Your waveform is played once, from the start of the run, and is zero after it ends. Its average is removed first, because a source with a non-zero average leaves a charge behind. A waveform with sharper detail than the grid can carry is refused, naming the highest frequency the grid resolves.

A smaller cell size resolves finer detail but makes the run much larger: halving it is about sixteen times the work. The app refuses a run that would be too big or too long before it is queued, and says which limit was hit; it also refuses a source or probe that lands in a metal, outside the grid or inside the absorbing layer at its edge.
